Not a standard CRM setup

One project can involve an architect, builder, engineer and applicator. One company can sit on many projects.

Each product family had its own sales route. All of this had to fit in a CRM simple enough for a mixed team to use.

What we started with

One person held the process

Orders, stock, inspections and warranties ran on memory and hand-offs.

CRM and inventory apart

Quotes and orders were typed in twice.

Wrong data model

One company, one contact did not match real projects.

Messy legacy data

Two decades of history, changing IDs and hidden warranty values.

Files and inspections adrift

Reports and photos had to be found and linked by hand.

Relationships before records

Simplified relationship model. The site project sits at the centre.

Opportunity / site project
  • Specifiers
  • Builders
  • Engineers
  • Applicators and contractors
  • Product line
  • Inspections
  • Warranties
  • Orders, stock and invoices

Simple on the surface

Early stage-heavy designs felt too complex in testing, so we swapped them for clear action buttons.

  • Six lead flows behind one set of action buttons
  • Stock shown as a simple traffic light
  • Best-match pricing in one click, with approval above a threshold
  • Inspections started from the project, report linked back

What changed

Delivered / observed

  • A data model built around real projects and many participants
  • Guided lead flows with simpler user actions
  • Opportunities and projects unified around the site
  • Pricing, discount approval, contractor matching and warranty tracking in CRM
  • Migration of about 14,000 contacts, 6,700 companies and 6,400+ opportunities and projects
  • Role-based guides, test cases and training material

Proposal-stage time and savings estimates were not measured after go-live, so none are shown.

Design the exception path

An integration is not done when one API call works. Users need to see when data did not arrive, and why.
